# RHEED Method
Much like the x-ray diffraction discussed in the previous assignment, the RHEED (reflection high energy electron diffraction) is a crystallographic method used for the investigation of the structure of crystalline solids. Unlike the x-ray diffraction though, and as its name suggests, it relies on the diffraction of electrons and not of electromagnetic radiation. It also differs from the x-ray diffraction in the sense that it probes the structure of the surface of materials and not their bulk. The underlying principle is identical - due to the wave nature of the electron, diffraction off the surface of materials results in a diffraction pattern with peaks of constructive interference at certain angles that correspond to the structure of the reciprocal lattice, from it the direct lattice can be easily calculated.

![center|500](../9.%20Misc/attachments/Pasted%20image%2020240120221000.png)

The figure above illustrates a generic RHEED setup. By shooting electrons from an electron gun at a glancing angle relative to the surface of the material, electrons only interact with the surface of the material and manage to escape. The scattered electrons fall on a phosphorescent screen behind the sample which and mark the location of collision. There are two types of scattering events, elastic scattering (kinematic) and inelastic scattering (dynamic) during which the electron loses energy to the crystal. Both can contain information about the composition of the surface, but we'll focus on the kinematic scattering.

### Inferring the Structure of the Surface
In previous assignment I briefly mentioned Laue condition for constructive interference - equivalent to Bragg's condition - which states that the change in wave-vector (of the scattering wave) must equal a lattice vector. Mathematically its written as 

$$
\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{k'} = \mathbf{G}
$$
where $\mathbf{k}$ is the incoming wave vector, $\mathbf{k'}$ is that of the scattered wave and $\mathbf{G}$ is the reciprocal lattice vector. ![right|350](../9.%20Misc/attachments/3-s2.0-B9780128111666000054-f05-05-9780128111666.jpg)A useful construction, the Ewald sphere, is a sphere in reciprocal space, with a radius equal to the magnitude of the incoming wave vector, and the head of the wave vector at its center. Since we consider the kineatic scattering, momentum is conserved and the surface of the sphere contains all "allowed" scattering wave vectors.  Together with Laue condition, the "allowed" scattering states reduce to reciprocal lattice points that intersect the sphere. Or in the case of the 2D lattice that is the surface of the material, reciprocal lattice points become rods as shown in the figure. The lit points on the phosphorescent can be traced back and their intersection with the imaginary Ewald sphere can be calculated, and consequently the $\mathbf{k'}$ vectors which together with $\mathbf{k}$ give the lattice vectors. The Ewald sphere radius can be changed by changing the De-Broglie wavelength of the electron (changing their momentum) and this wave "scan" more lattice rods. Alternatively, the direction of incidence can also be changed so that the sphere "sweeps" more lattice rods.

### RHEED Oscillations
![right|400](../9.%20Misc/attachments/rheed1.jpg)Another use of the RHEED method can be found in the MBE as discussed in class. By locking the system on a point of constructive interference, the width of the layer can be probed in-situ while growing the crystal. The image on the right illustrates nicely the decline in RHEED signal as the layer starts to build and becomes less homogenous. The signal peaks again when an atomic layer has been added and the surface is yet again homogenous. From the separation between the peaks, and the changed angle of constructive interference, the effective width of the layer can be inferred.
