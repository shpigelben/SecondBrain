The equivalence principle postulates that there is no difference between gravitational mass (mass that is determined by the how a massive particle moves under the influence of gravity), and inertial mass (determined by collisions of two particles). 

# Gravity = Acceleration
___
$$ F_g = m_g g $$
$$ F_I = m_I a $$
There exists no experiment that can distinguish between an accelerating frame $a=g$ and a frame that is subjected to a constant gravitational field $g$. That means that the same force is measured in both frames, this leads to the conclusion that
$$ m_I = m_g $$

and more enlightening is the fact that __locally__, gravity and acceleration cannot be distinguished

![[../9. Misc/attachments/Equivalence Principle.png|center|600]]

The equivalence principle is a "simple" conclusion with remarkable consequences. One of the many remarkable consequences of the equivalence principle is the fact that gravity affects massless rays of light.

If one were to take frame 1 (depicted in the image above) and consider the trajectory drawn in blue as the trajectory of a light beam which enters the frame perpendicular to the motion of the frame, it would seem that light deserves no special treatment for being massless, and due to the equivalence principle, one would be correct to conclude that gravity changes the trajectory of light.

This effect has been confirmed experimentally and is used in gravitational lensing extensively. In fact it was the confirmation of this fact that led to the acceptance of general relativity as a viable theory in the scientific community.

# Locality
___
The equivalence principle allows us to treat acceleration as gravity and vice versa, so long as they are both constant. The indistinguishabillity of the two in non constant gravitational field holds for infinitesimally small frames where variation in small. We say that in curved spacetime, the equivalence principle holds for tangent flat spaces and so the equivalence principle holds for constant accelerations in flat [[Minkowski Space|spacetime]]. This fits well with the notion that an observer in an [[Intrinsic & Extrinsic Geometry|intrinsic]] geometry cannot measure its extrinsic characteristics.
