# answers

## muscle balance

*(revised to weight by actual intensity per exercise, not just rep count, and constrained to what's on hand: one 16kg KB, two 5kg dumbbells, bodyweight/pullup bar)*

- intensity-adjusted, the pullups (5 reps, full bodyweight, vertical pull) are probably already close to your per-set effort ceiling — likely *harder* than the 10 pushups, not easier despite fewer reps. so the imbalance isn't really "not enough pulling reps," it's "no horizontal pulling **plane** at all." pullups only train vertical pull (lats, biceps); pushups are horizontal push (chest, front delts, triceps). nothing in the circuit trains horizontal pull (rhomboids, mid-traps, rear delts) — that's the direct antagonist to the pushup pattern and the most likely reason the pecs/front delts are pulling ahead
- similarly, 16kg is fairly light for a KB deadlift (5 reps there is low-intensity for that pattern) and 5kg lateral raises are light-isolation, so those two aren't doing much heavy lifting either way — there's room to swap/add without needing more equipment
- **three additions, all doable with what you already own:**
  - **single-arm KB rows**, 8/side, using the 16kg — bent over, row the KB into the hip. this is the direct fix: a horizontal pull loaded heavy enough to matter, using the same tool you already use for squats/deadlifts. put it right after pushups in the circuit so the plane-pairing is explicit
  - **bent-over reverse flyes**, 12–15 reps, with the two 5kg dumbbells — light load is actually ideal here since it's an isolation/postural movement (rear delts, mid-traps), not a strength one. pairs against the lateral raises the same way rows pair against pushups
  - **KB swings**, 15–20 reps, using the 16kg, as a replacement or addition to the deadlift — same hip-hinge pattern but ballistic, so it turns a low-intensity movement into a genuinely demanding one, and it spikes heart rate far more than a slow deadlift does (relevant below for the HIIT/BDNF angle). if you keep both, alternate deadlifts and swings across sessions rather than stacking all that hip-hinge volume in one circuit
- net result — a rebalanced circuit with no new equipment: 5 pullups → 10 elevated pushups → **8/side single-arm KB rows** → 10 goblet squats → **15–20 KB swings** (or 5 KB deadlifts) → 5 side-ups/side → 10 lateral raises → **12–15 reverse flyes**
- still genuinely neglected after this: calves and rotator cuff/external rotation — jump rope (below) covers the calves for free; rotator cuff work needs a band, which is the one cheap piece of equipment worth adding if you expand your kit later

## is this HIIT?

- as written (just a list of exercises/reps, no timing), this is a **strength/resistance circuit**, not classic HIIT. true HIIT is defined by structured work:rest intervals at near-maximal cardiovascular effort (e.g. 30–45s hard / 15–20s rest), usually with movements chosen for continuous large-muscle output
- however: if you run it circuit-style — minimal rest between exercises (15–30s), multiple rounds back to back — it functions as **High-Intensity Circuit Training (HICT)**, a resistance-based cousin of HIIT. studies on bodyweight/resistance circuits show HICT can hit similar heart-rate zones and produce comparable EPOC ("afterburn") to sprint-style HIIT
- to make it more genuinely HIIT-like: tighten rest intervals, do 3+ rounds continuously, and keep effort close to failure on each set — the exercise selection itself is fine, it's the interval structure that determines whether it's HIIT vs. straight strength work
- since you're already doing it with minimal rest between sets: that confirms it, you're running HICT, not plain strength work. that matters for the BDNF question below — the minimal-rest style is exactly what drives the lactate spike that seems to matter most

## HIIT, BDNF, and brain benefits

- **intensity is the main lever** — near-maximal effort produces more lactate, and lactate itself appears to act as a signaling molecule that crosses the blood-brain barrier and upregulates BDNF (this is fairly well-supported mechanistically). moderate/easy effort blunts this — push the hard intervals genuinely hard
- **consistency > any single session** — BDNF spikes from one workout are transient (peaks ~30–60 min post-exercise, back to baseline within hours). the durable cognitive benefits (e.g. hippocampal volume, memory) come from regular training over weeks/months, not one good session
- **learn/study in the post-workout window** — neuroplasticity and BDNF are elevated for roughly 1–2 hours after a hard session, so doing cognitively demanding work (studying, skill acquisition) in that window may enhance consolidation better than doing it beforehand
- **fasted training** — some evidence that training fasted increases ketone bodies (e.g. beta-hydroxybutyrate), which independently upregulate BDNF gene expression. this is a real pathway but less robustly established than intensity itself — worth experimenting with (e.g. training before breakfast) but don't force it if it tanks your performance/intensity, since intensity is the bigger lever
- **sleep is a hard requirement, not optional** — a lot of the synaptic-plasticity benefit of exercise-driven BDNF is consolidated during sleep; skimping on sleep after training likely blunts the cognitive payoff
- **nutritional cofactors (weaker evidence, still reasonable)**: omega-3s (DHA/EPA), polyphenol-rich foods (berries, dark cocoa), caffeine pre-workout (mainly by letting you train harder, hence more lactate/BDNF stimulus indirectly)
- **things I'd flag as more speculative**: time-of-day effects beyond fasted-state considerations, and cold exposure post-workout (norepinephrine spike has some tenuous BDNF association, but it's thin evidence) — wouldn't prioritize these over intensity, consistency, and sleep

## rope jumping for BDNF

- as a HIIT tool on its own, jump rope is excellent: cheap, portable, spins up heart rate almost immediately, and is naturally suited to interval structure (e.g. 30–40s max-pace skipping / 15–20s rest, 6–10 rounds). that's a clean way to hit the intensity/lactate lever from the section above without needing any equipment at all
- there's a specific angle beyond plain cardio intensity: jump rope also carries a **coordination/motor-skill demand** (rhythm, timing, footwork) on top of the cardiovascular load. some research on "coordinative" or complex-movement exercise (dance, agility drills, skill-based cardio) suggests it may add benefits — particularly for executive function — beyond what steady repetitive cardio (e.g. running, cycling) provides on its own, because it recruits motor-learning/cerebellar circuits alongside the aerobic BDNF pathway. this evidence is thinner and more mixed than the core intensity findings, so treat it as a genuine bonus rather than the main reason to do it
- practically, two ways to use it against your circuit:
  - **as a standalone HIIT session** on non-lifting days — good if you want dedicated interval cardio without touching the KB/DB
  - **as a finisher right after the circuit** — 3–5 min of interval skipping while you're already warm and fatigued pushes heart rate into a higher zone at exactly the point of peak metabolic stress, likely maximizing the lactate/BDNF stimulus from the section above, more so than doing it cold
- bonus: it directly trains calves, which is the one muscle group flagged as genuinely neglected in the circuit above — so it's a two-for-one (BDNF + the muscle-balance gap) rather than a pure add-on
- caution: higher-impact on ankles/calves than the rest of the current bodyweight work — build up volume gradually if you're not used to skipping, rather than jumping straight to daily 5-minute sessions
