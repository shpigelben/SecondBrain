# Thoughts on a Classical Analogue
The uncertainty principle which states that, for example, space and momentum (which are conjugate variables) cannot be completely known at the same time. This is known to be a quality exclusive to the quantum realm, but a classical analogue of such measurement scenario raises deep questions about the nature of knowledge, measurements and correspondence between the quantum and classical realms.

Consider a car speeding down the highway. In order to measure that car's velocity (which is synonymous with momentum for the sake of this discussion) one would have to at least wait for a time interval for the car to travel further and then calculate the velocity based on that displacement.

$$
v = \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}
$$
The shorter the time interval the more accurate the instantaneous velocity and the smaller the displacement. And in a sense, in the limit of infinitesimal time interval the velocity and the position of the car can be simultaneously known to infinite accuracy.

But if we assume that time interval for measurement cannot be infinitesimal (can it be in practice? what about relativistic considerations?) and is rather finite. It makes sense that the greater velocity we measure, the greater our uncertainty of the car's location, since during the time interval it covered a certain distance. We ultimately know point A and point B of that journey, and those, together with the time interval, give us the velocity. But it is clear now that greater velocity means greater variance in the car's location.
