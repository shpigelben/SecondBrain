The [[Gravitational Time Dilation]] presents another related effect. That of gravitational Redshift. When moving from a place of high gravitational potential to a place of lower gravitational potential, a light beam will be Redshited that is, its wavelength "stretch" and the light will have a lower wavelength than it did when it left the stronger gravity.

We have derived the equation for gravitational time dilation in the following formula

  $$\Delta t_B = \Delta t_A\left( 1- \frac{\Phi_A-\Phi_B}{c^2} \right) = \Delta t_A\left( 1- \frac{GM}{c^2}\left[\frac{1}{R_B}-\frac{1}{R_A}\right] \right)$$
  
  Frequency is inversely proportional to time, so if we consider the frequency $\omega$ of a light beam, the dilation effect should work inversely
  
  $$ \omega_A=\omega_B \left( 1- \frac{\Phi_A-\Phi_B}{c^2} \right) = \omega_B\left( 1- \frac{GM}{c^2}\left[\frac{1}{R_B}-\frac{1}{R_A}\right] \right) $$
  
  So if we take $\omega_B \mapsto \omega$ to the frequency of a signal that is sent from the radius $(R_B \mapsto R)$ of a planet $(M)$, and we measure its frequency $\omega_A\mapsto \omega_{\infty}$ far enough $(R_A \mapsto \infty)$ so that the gravitational field of the star is negligible, we get
  
> $$\
 \omega_{\infty} =  \omega\left( 1- \frac{GM}{Rc^2}\right)
 $$
