The measurement problem is most famously known in the quantum-mechanical context whereby the collapse of the wave-function is induced by a mysterious measurement. While it is perhaps one of the central themes in modern day quantum research, the problem to which I refer here as the "classical" measurement problem, has nothing to do with it.

The measurements that I refer to have nothing to do with wave-functions, observables or quantum uncertainties, but with the "ordinary" measurement of classical phenomena. And the question that I want to pose is - "what fundamental quantities do we actually measure when we claim to have measured a certain quantity?" For example, it is said that potentials are mathematical tools rather than physical entities since the don't directly interact with our measurement apparatus. If we consider the electric field, sure it is easy to agree to the claim that electric potential is a mathematical tool that can be back engineered from the field, but why exactly is the field more physical? Don't we exercise an analogous back-engineering when considering it?

A field is said to have a physical reality since we can take a charge ($q$), put it in an electric field  and watch how it accelerates according to it
$$\mathbf{a} = \frac{q\mathbf{E}}{m} \to \mathbf{E} = \frac{m\mathbf{a}}{q}$$
But what do we actually measure here? Well, acceleration, which is simply the position of the charge as it changes in time. We fundamentally have rulers and and watches that allows us to calculate the electric field. There is no special field detector that can magically tell the field value, it is all inferred. Why is it then that the electric field is more physical than the potential if, like the potential, we use mathematics to back-engineer it's value?

Maybe because of the extra steps it takes to calculate the potential or maybe because it requires a little fancier math? The most physically correct answer would be that the potential is not unique in its definition, there are multiple potentials that correspond to the physical phenomenon (or the field) that we observe, but the field is unique.

==Back to the question of measuring different physical quantities - if we look hard enough, it becomes clear that no matter what type of quantity one tries to measure , one fundamentally measures length and time intervals.==
